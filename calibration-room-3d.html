How calibration works

The freedom. A flat image can't see depth on its own — depth is a direction the image group is blind to. Cross-slit imaging hands you two references at two depths (the slits, ℓ₁ and ℓ₂), and nominating a screen adds a third. Three fixed marks on every ray, plus the object, make four — and four points on a line have a cross-ratio.

The three acts of calibrating

1 · Nominate the screen. Fixing the picture plane is gauge-fixing: it turns the invisible depth gauge into an observable. The vanishing point — where every depth line converges — is the screen's image of the point at infinity.

2 · Fix the metric. The two slit depths a, b are the calibration constants. They set the aspect-ratio-to-depth law AR(Z) = a(Z−b)/(b(Z−a)) and the curvature of the depth lines, which grows with the gap D = b − a.

3 · Read. Now every projected frame's aspect ratio is the cross-ratio (ℓ₁, ℓ₂ ; screen, Z) along its ray — a measured depth. Invert it (a Möbius map) and the image becomes metric 3D. The panel closes this loop live: front-frame aspect → decoded Z → the room's true front depth.

Why the depth lines curve

A single pinhole scales x and y by the same factor, so depth lines run straight out of the vanishing point — ordinary one-point perspective. Two slits at two depths scale x and y by different factors, so a depth line bends: its aspect drifts with depth. The bend is the calibration made visible. Merge the slits (a = b) and the two factors coincide — lines straighten, every frame images square (AR ≡ 1), and the single center can no longer tell one depth from another. Cross-slit calibration works precisely where a lone pinhole is blind.
